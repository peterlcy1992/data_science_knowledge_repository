# When LLM-Based User Profiling Adds Value in Production Streaming Recommendation

**Source:** [arXiv (Comcast Technology AI / DePaul University)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.27183) · Published 2026-09 · Added 2026-09-25
**Category:** Personalization & Recommender Systems · **Tags:** `user-profiling`, `llm`, `recommendation`, `evaluation`, `cost-benefit`

## TL;DR

A production-data study from Comcast Technology AI and DePaul University factorially compares aggregate user-profile representations (numerical aggregates of item embeddings) against LLM-generated natural-language user profiles, crossed with two ways of handling recency (recent vs. historical behavior), for streaming recommendation. The framing is explicitly cost/benefit rather than advocacy: LLM-based profiling costs meaningfully more to generate than aggregate embeddings, so the paper asks when — for which user segments and which quality dimensions — that extra cost is actually worth it.

## 1. Business context

User profiling — building a compact representation of what a person likes, based on their history — underlies most personalization systems, and teams building streaming-recommendation systems face a real choice about how to build that representation: cheap, well-understood aggregate methods (averaging or otherwise combining the embeddings of items a user has engaged with) versus LLM-based methods that generate a natural-language summary of a user's taste and encode that text instead. LLM-based profiling is a popular pattern in current recommender-system research and practice, but it's also significantly more expensive to run at scale than aggregate embedding math — which raises a direct production question this paper takes on: is the extra representational richness of an LLM-written profile worth its extra cost, and if so, under what conditions?

## 2. Technical details

The study is structured as a factorial comparison: two representation types (aggregate — numerical combination of semantic item embeddings; LLM-based — a natural-language summary of user preferences generated by an LLM and then encoded via a text encoder) crossed with different temporal-handling strategies that disentangle a user's recent behavior from their longer historical behavior. This produces four profiling strategies evaluated head-to-head on a real production streaming-recommendation dataset, measured across both accuracy metrics and "beyond-accuracy" dimensions of recommendation quality (the public abstract does not specify which beyond-accuracy metrics, e.g., diversity or novelty, were used). The explicit research question the authors pose is when the added cost of LLM-based profile generation is justified — i.e., under which user-behavior types and quality dimensions the natural-language representation actually outperforms the cheaper aggregate approach, rather than assuming it always does.

## 3. Impact — potential & realized

The publicly available abstract frames the comparison and the motivating cost/benefit question clearly but does not itself state which strategy wins under which conditions — that result lives in the full paper. What's notable and worth recording here is the framing itself: a rigorous, production-data-grounded cost/benefit study of a currently-fashionable pattern (LLM-generated user profiles) is a useful corrective to the more common "LLMs improve personalization" narrative that often skips the cost side of the comparison entirely.

### Jargon buster

- **User profiling** — building a compact representation of a user's preferences (numeric vector or text summary) from their interaction history, used as an input signal to a recommendation model.
- **Factorial comparison** — an experimental design that tests multiple factors (here, representation type and temporal handling) in all combinations, so their individual and combined effects can be isolated rather than confounded.
- **Beyond-accuracy metrics** — recommendation-quality measures that go beyond simple prediction accuracy, such as diversity, novelty, serendipity, or coverage across a catalog.
