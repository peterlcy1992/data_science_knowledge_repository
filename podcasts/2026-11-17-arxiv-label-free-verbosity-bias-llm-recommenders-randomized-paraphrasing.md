# University of Granada — Does your AI shopping assistant prefer wordier products?

When a large language model picks one product out of five for a shopper, it should be choosing on relevance — but does it also reward sellers who simply write more? This paper from the University of Granada shows how to audit that without a single relevance label. The trick is borrowed from experiment design: rewrite each candidate's description with a randomly assigned paraphrasing model, shuffle the order, and check whether the recommender picks every paraphraser equally often, which it must if it ignores wording. We walk through the four-step statistical pipeline, from a chi-squared uniformity test to a conditional-logit model that separates description length from relevance, and the results across 20,000 Amazon product queries and nine open models: seven of nine depart from uniform selection, and in all nine a longer description raises the odds of being chosen after controlling for measured relevance — by about 12% to 38% per standard deviation of length. Relevance still wins when the two signals conflict, but by less than half the usual margin. We close on what it costs to run this as a routine audit when you swap a model or a prompt, and what the method can and can't rule out. Source article: "Label-Free Detection of Structural Bias in LLM-Based Recommender Systems via Randomized Paraphrasing: The Case of Verbosity" — University of Granada / DaSCI (arXiv cs.IR), https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18878 (published 2026-11).
