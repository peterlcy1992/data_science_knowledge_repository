## 🔎 Deep dive: Agent Evaluation Without Ground Truth: A Null Proportion Perspective

*Cho (Cornell Tech), Lindon (Netflix) & Kallus (Cornell Tech / Netflix) · arXiv cs.AI, cross-listed stat.ME / stat.AP, 23 Nov 2026 · llm-genai (AI/ML-systems) · AI/ML pick (rotation: 2 of the last 4 deep dives were Core DS, so AI/ML-systems was due) · novelty 4/5 · full text read*

**The problem.** Benchmarks like SWE-Bench grade an agent's decisions with unit tests. But an agent that ships a UI change to lift engagement is graded by a noisy experiment, and whether the change truly helped, "a statement about the unknown distribution of the system", is never observed. LLM judges are biased and give invalid intervals. Prediction-powered inference needs at least a few true labels, and here there are none. Real deployments also break classical null-proportion methods: decisions adapt to earlier outcomes, monitoring can stop at any time, and the quality metric (e.g. "share of adopted changes that helped") is only defined after the run.

**The method.**
- **Estimand.** Agent quality θ* = Σ cᵢθᵢ, a weighted count of unobserved correct decisions. The weights may depend on the whole trajectory: cᵢ = 1/M gives the share of correct changes. One example counts "safe improvements": a ≥10% lift in the goal metric with no worse guard metric.
- **Evidence = e-values, not labels.** Each decision gets two e-value streams: one tests "it was correct", the other "it was wrong". E-values survive optional stopping and can be combined under arbitrary dependence. Sequential A/B and release-gating systems already compute them.
- **Bounds.** ψᵢ = max(cᵢ − Aᵢ, −Āᵢ) built from predictably weighted evidence sums. Summed, they give an upper bound valid in expectation over *every* trajectory distribution (Theorem 1), which can still get arbitrarily close to the sharpest achievable bound (Theorem 2). With one e-value per decision, a member of the class beats Storey's estimator pathwise (Lemma 1).
- **Confidence sets.** Keep every candidate label vector whose "wealth" stays below 1/α; the endpoints solve a 0–1 knapsack. Validity needs **shared wealth**: one betting budget across all candidate vectors. In the paper's false-alarm example (an agent ships a bad "fix" after a spurious alarm), separate weights miss with probability 0.145 at α = 0.1, shared weights with 0.045.
- **Tuning.** 64 simulated continuations per step choose the weights. A wrong simulator can widen the intervals but cannot break validity.

**Case study.** Claude Opus 5 and Claude Haiku 4.5 (high effort) each made 50 sequential, dependent runtime optimizations to NetworkX. Each change was timed against its parent on random graphs with seeds the agents never saw, in 14 workload families. Right-Haar t-style e-processes; sampling stopped when either e-process hit 100 or after 150 pairs. At a **5% minimum speedup, Opus's estimated lower bound on the share of correct changes is ≈0.15, above Haiku's estimated upper bound of 0.11**. Opus is higher at every threshold. Both agents' 90% pointwise confidence sets stay above zero even at 10%. The authors call this "a case study, rather than a rigorous evaluation".

**Claude's Take (opinion).** The building blocks are known (e-values, martingale merging, Storey-style null-proportion bounds). The new part is combining them for agent trajectories, where the labels are random and produced by the agent itself, and the false-alarm example shows that is a real change to the problem. For data scientists the key move is to treat agent evaluation as an **experimentation problem, not a labelling problem**. An agent shipping changes is a stream of sequential A/B tests, and its quality is a false-discovery-style quantity that teams with anytime-valid testing could compute from logs they already have. Caveats: one task, one run per agent, pointwise intervals across thresholds, results mostly in a figure, asymptotic (not exact) e-processes for non-Gaussian timing noise, and deliberately conservative intervals when decisions are few.
