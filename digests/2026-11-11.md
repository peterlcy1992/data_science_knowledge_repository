## 🔎 Deep dive: Exploiting Feature-invariant Scaling Laws for Efficient A/B Experimentation in Recommendation Systems

*arXiv cs.IR (Meta: Zhiliang Chen, Sammy Nammari, Jiahao Su, Xiaoqiang Luo) · personalization-recsys (AI/ML, tied to DS experimentation and decision rules) · AI/ML pick (rotation: the last 4 deep dives included 2 Core DS, so AI/ML-systems was due) · novelty 4/5 · full text read*

**The problem.** Recommender teams decide whether a model change (architecture, optimizer or data feature) helps by retraining at full scale and comparing. The paper calls this A/B experimentation, but it is offline model comparison on validation loss, not a randomized online test. Full retrains are expensive and must be repeated for every candidate change, which the authors say can number in the hundreds. The common shortcut, one small-scale run, is often misleading. In their motivating case, adding movie genre information to an LLM-based recommender's prompt lowers HR@10 at small scale but significantly improves it at full scale on MovieLens-20M. You also cannot choose a "safe" small scale in advance, because different kinds of changes give different signals at different small scales.

**The idea.** They call it *feature-invariant scaling*. A model with a change applied still follows a power law, Loss = a·N^b·D^c (N is model size, D is data size), of the same functional form as the baseline but with different coefficients. In the motivating case both fits have R² > 0.99. So you run a sweep of small co-scaled experiments, fit the log-linear scaling regression and compute the closed-form predictive distribution of full-scale loss. The paper notes that flat-prior Bayesian and frequentist prediction intervals coincide here. The decision rule: call a change beneficial if Pr(full-scale loss < 0.99 × baseline | small runs) > 90%. The margin drops to 0.05% for Wukong and the LLM model, whose effects are below 1%. A sweep at 0.1 to 0.5 scale costs about 55% of one full run. Where the curve is not a power law, they describe an MCMC variant.

**The evidence.** They tested three model families: Wukong, HSTU, and GR2, an LLM-based recommender fine-tuned from Qwen3-Instruct and Llama-3.1-Instruct, across more than 40 architectural, optimization and data changes. As small-scale points are added, the posterior probability of improvement rises toward 100% for truly helpful changes and falls toward zero for regressions, and the out-of-sample error of the full-scale prediction shrinks. Changes the method flagged improved HSTU-Large on MovieLens-20M: HR@10 rose from 0.3567 to as high as 0.3603 (+1.01%), HR@50 from 0.6139 to 0.6186, and HR@200 from 0.8073 to 0.8112. They also report failures of the power-law fit, such as Qwen-0.5B after genre information was added.

**Claude's Take (opinion).** This turns "is this model change good?" into a predictive-inference question with an explicit threshold and a posterior probability. It is the offline-model counterpart of surrogate-based early readouts in online experiments, and the authors cite Athey et al.'s surrogate index. Caveats:
- The intervals hold only if the power-law form holds, and the paper shows cases where it doesn't.
- Validation loss is still a proxy for the online metric.
- At about 55% of a full run, this is a screen for which changes deserve a full retrain, not a replacement for one.
