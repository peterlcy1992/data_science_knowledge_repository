## 🔎 Deep Dive: AI Changed How Spotify Builds — What We Learned (and Fixed) About Quality at Higher Velocity

**Source:** [Spotify Engineering](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2026/9/ai-changed-how-spotify-builds-what-we-learned-and-fixed-about-quality-at-higher-velocity) · Published 2026-09
**Category:** LLMs & Generative AI

### TL;DR

As AI-assisted and agentic coding roughly doubled Spotify's merged-change volume year over year, quality problems that used to surface slowly started compounding faster than review, testing, rollout, and observability systems could catch them. Spotify's investigation found AI-authored code was not the direct cause — the surrounding delivery infrastructure hadn't been built for the new velocity — and walks through fixes across four incident classes: content-processing delays, fleet-update failures, compute shortages, and mobile quality regressions.

### Business context

Total merged changes at Spotify more than doubled year over year in August (roughly 8,100 to 17,000), and the share of that work classified as quality/optimization work rose from 27% to 31% — meaning engineers put more than twice as much absolute effort into quality work as the year before. Yet a cluster of production incidents still emerged: media content processing fell behind, automated fleet updates began causing production failures, compute capacity ran short during peak load, and mobile app quality regressions accumulated faster than existing detection systems could flag them. The risk was that AI coding velocity would get blamed and throttled, when the actual gap was between how fast code could now be produced and how fast the surrounding delivery systems — monitoring, rollout safety, capacity planning, regression detection — could absorb it.

### Technical details

Spotify tracked an age-weighted rework-rate metric (not raw code churn, which rises mechanically with AI-assisted editing) and found no corresponding rise relative to the industry-wide code-churn increase — no evidence of accumulating "AI-induced quality debt." Each incident class traced back to delivery infrastructure that hadn't scaled with throughput:

- **Content processing** — end-to-end monitoring, fixed scheduling bugs, workload prioritization, more transcoding capacity.
- **Fleet updates** — stronger rollout safeguards, expanded rollback capacity, riskier changes scheduled during team working hours.
- **Compute management** — doubled reserved edge capacity, failover redesigned to gracefully degrade lower-tier services under load.
- **Mobile quality** — broader quality signal metrics, longer-term trend analysis to catch slow-accumulating regressions before they become incidents.

The throughline: treat AI-driven velocity as a capacity-planning and observability problem, not a code-authorship problem.

### Impact

**Realized:** merged changes roughly doubled (8,100 → 17,000 in August) while the rework-rate metric showed no corresponding increase — no measurable rise in AI-induced rework relative to the broader code-churn trend.

**Potential:** a useful case study for any organization scaling agentic-coding adoption — the failure mode isn't necessarily code quality, but the delivery and observability systems built for a slower cadence. Rework-rate (not raw churn) is offered as a better AI-quality signal, and the piece argues against reflexively tightening review thresholds in response to a volume increase.

### Claude's Take

**Novelty: 3/5 — solid production learnings, not a new technique.** There's no new model or architecture here — this is an engineering-culture and reliability retrospective. What makes it worth reading is the discipline of separating "AI wrote more code" from "AI wrote worse code," and choosing a metric (age-weighted rework rate) that actually tests that distinction rather than reaching for the more alarming but less precise raw-churn number. It's a production-first, empirically grounded take on a question a lot of organizations are currently answering with anecdote.
